# Persuasion and Behavioral Principles

Documented behavioral-science and psychology research, used to generate angle hypotheses at method step 2. This is external research literature, not this business's evidence. Classify per [`KNOWLEDGE-TAXONOMY.md`](../_shared/KNOWLEDGE-TAXONOMY.md) as documented research or a framework/model — never as a proven method for this account. A principle establishing that a mechanism exists in general does not establish that a specific execution moves this audience; that remains an untested claim until the creative matrix tests it.

## How to use this reference

A principle below is a candidate lens for forming an angle hypothesis, not a template to fill in. Select a principle because it fits the actual audience situation, tension, and mechanism identified in method step 1 — not because a checklist says every campaign needs a scarcity angle. Most executions should draw on one or two principles clearly, not stack many at once; stacking usually produces a confused message, not a more persuasive one.

Every claim generated from a principle still passes through this skill's existing rule: match proof strength to claim strength, do not imply guaranteed, clinical, financial, or comparative outcomes without substantiation. A behavioral principle is never a substitute for evidence a specific claim requires.

## Principles

**Social proof.** People weight a choice more credible when they see others like them have made it. Fits when genuine, verifiable adoption or satisfaction evidence exists (review volume, customer count, usage data). Do not fabricate or imply a volume of adoption the business cannot substantiate; a specific number requires a specific source.

**Authority.** People weight a claim more credible when it comes from a source with relevant, legitimate expertise. Fits when a genuine credential, certification, or expert endorsement exists. Do not imply an unearned credential or borrow authority the source did not actually grant; `AGENTS.md` prohibits fabricating credentials outright.

**Scarcity and urgency.** People weight an option more valuable when availability or time is limited. Fits only when the limitation is real — genuine stock, capacity, or deadline constraints. A fabricated countdown or manufactured "limited" claim is a dark pattern and a trust liability, not a creative technique; route any hard authorization question about a live claim's honesty to human judgment rather than assuming a persuasive framing is acceptable because it is common practice.

**Loss aversion.** People weigh the pain of a potential loss more heavily than the pleasure of an equivalent gain (prospect theory). Fits a message reframing an existing cost, risk, or missed opportunity the audience already has, rather than only a new benefit being offered. Do not construct a loss that does not genuinely exist for the audience being addressed.

**Anchoring.** An initial number or reference point shapes how later numbers are judged, even when the anchor is arbitrary. Fits pricing or value-comparison messaging where a genuine reference point (a real prior price, a real comparable alternative) exists. Do not present an inflated or fabricated reference price as the anchor; a manufactured anchor is a substantiation failure, not a technique.

**Reciprocity.** People feel obligated to return a favor or value received. Fits an offer genuinely providing value upfront (a real free resource, a real trial, real assistance) before asking for the desired action. The value offered has to be real and delivered, not a nominal gesture designed only to trigger the obligation.

**Commitment and consistency.** People act consistently with commitments and self-image they have already expressed, even a small one. Fits a sequence where an earlier, small, genuine action (an opt-in, a stated preference, a quiz answer) informs a later, larger ask — a lifecycle-sequence consideration to route through `$lifecycle-marketing` for sequencing, with this skill supplying the angle and message.

**Unity / in-group identity.** People are more persuaded by an appeal from a group they identify as belonging to. Fits when the audience segment per `$icp-jtbd` has a genuine shared identity, situation, or affiliation the message can speak to authentically. Do not construct or imply a shared identity that does not reflect the actual audience.

**Dual-process framing (System 1 / System 2).** People process some decisions quickly and heuristically (low involvement, low stakes, high familiarity) and others slowly and deliberately (high involvement, high stakes, unfamiliar). Fits informing format and proof depth to the funnel stage and purchase involvement: a low-involvement impulse category can lean on a fast, emotionally resonant hook; a high-consideration purchase typically needs more substantive proof and detail before the ask, regardless of how persuasive the opening hook is.

**Decoy and framing effects.** How options are presented and compared (which alternatives are shown alongside the offer, which attributes are emphasized) shapes the perceived value of a choice independent of the choice's actual merits. Fits offer-presentation and comparison-page work; route to `$cro` for the actual page-level implementation, this skill supplies the underlying message and comparison framing.

**Endowment effect.** People value something more once they perceive they already possess or are entitled to it. Fits trial, sample, or "already yours until you decide" framings, where the perception of possession is genuine (an active trial, a real hold) rather than manufactured.

## Rules

- A principle explains a general mechanism; it does not predict this audience's specific response. Treat every resulting angle as a hypothesis requiring the test matrix, per this skill's existing method — never as pre-validated because the underlying psychology is well-documented.
- Do not use a principle to justify a claim, comparison, or urgency signal that is not genuinely true; the principle names a persuasive mechanism, it does not relax the proof-strength requirement this skill already enforces on every claim.
- Do not stack more than one or two principles into a single execution without a clear reason; a message trying to invoke scarcity, social proof, and authority simultaneously typically reads as manipulative rather than as more persuasive, and audiences increasingly recognize stacked persuasion tactics.
- Where a principle-driven tactic risks reading as manipulative or as a dark pattern regardless of factual accuracy (an artificially inflated countdown timer, a fabricated "X people are viewing this" claim, a fake scarcity signal), do not use it; factual accuracy is necessary but not sufficient, and a technically true but manipulative framing is still a trust and brand-risk decision requiring human judgment, not an automatic creative choice.
- Do not present this reference's principles as this business's proven patterns; a principle informs hypothesis generation at method step 2, and the resulting angle still needs the success signal, guardrail, and decision window this skill's method already requires before it is treated as evidence.
