# Strategic Framing and Angle Archetypes

Methods for arriving at a single clear strategic direction (method step 2's "distinct angle hypotheses") and archetypes for generating original angle directions rather than defaulting to the first idea. Read alongside [Persuasion and behavioral principles](persuasion-and-behavioral-principles.md), which supplies the psychological mechanism a chosen angle can draw on; this reference supplies the structuring method for reaching the angle itself.

Classify each method here as a framework or methodology per [`KNOWLEDGE-TAXONOMY.md`](../_shared/KNOWLEDGE-TAXONOMY.md). These are structuring methods for generating and organizing hypotheses — using one does not make the resulting angle proven; it still requires the test matrix.

## Strategic framing methods

**Problem, insight, advantage, strategy.** Work in this order: state the real audience problem or tension from method step 1's evidence; name the sharp insight — the non-obvious reason that problem persists or matters more than it appears to; identify this business's genuine, evidenced advantage relevant to that insight; only then commit to one strategic direction. Skipping to a direction before the insight is named usually produces generic creative; skipping the advantage check produces a direction the business cannot actually substantiate.

**Single-minded proposition.** State the one thing this creative must communicate, in one sentence, before producing anything. A brief or matrix cell that cannot be reduced to one proposition is not focused enough to test cleanly — this connects directly to the skill's existing matrix rule to vary one major dimension per learning cell; an unfocused proposition makes it unclear what the cell is even testing.

**Hypothesis statement format.** State each angle hypothesis as: "We believe [insight + angle] will move [specific metric] because [mechanism]." This is a stricter, more falsifiable form of the skill's existing requirement to form distinct angle hypotheses with a defined success signal — it forces the mechanism to be named explicitly, which makes the resulting test result easier to interpret (a null result tells you something about the stated mechanism, not just about "whether the ad worked").

**Motivation mapping.** Separate the audience's primal or emotional driver (status, security, belonging, desire, fear, identity) from the learned or rational justification they use to explain the decision to themselves and others. A strong angle often addresses both: the emotional driver for what actually moves the audience, the rational justification for what the audience needs to tell themselves and others to feel the decision was reasonable. An angle addressing only the rational layer frequently underperforms one that names the real emotional driver credibly and honestly.

## Angle archetypes

Starting directions for generating original angles beyond the first idea. Each is a lens to apply to the actual audience and product evidence from method step 1, not a template to fill in regardless of fit.

**Name a shared frustration.** Give the audience's existing, real frustration a name or shape, positioning the product as the response to it. Requires the frustration to be genuinely evidenced in customer language, not invented to manufacture a villain.

**Borrow cultural relevance.** Connect the product to a genuine, currently relevant cultural moment, trend, or reference the audience actually cares about. Requires real, current relevance — a stale or forced cultural reference reads as inauthentic and can date the creative quickly; confirm the reference is still live before producing against it.

**Create a new mental model.** Reframe how the audience thinks about the category or problem, offering a simplifying lens they did not have before. The strongest version of this archetype is genuinely useful independent of the sale — it changes how someone thinks, not just what they buy — but it also carries the highest bar for credibility, since an unconvincing reframe reads as marketing spin rather than insight.

**Challenge an accepted category norm.** Name something the category treats as normal or unquestioned and argue against it. Requires the challenged norm to be real and recognizable to the audience; challenging a norm no one actually holds falls flat, and the argument against it needs genuine substance, not just contrarian framing.

**Name an overlooked truth.** Surface something true about the category, product use, or audience situation that is rarely said explicitly, because it is either obvious in hindsight or slightly uncomfortable. Requires the truth to be genuinely true and verifiable, not a manufactured-sounding insight dressed up as candor.

## Rules

- A strategic framing method organizes how an angle is reached; it does not substitute for the audience, product, and evidence inputs method step 1 requires. Applying a framework to weak or missing inputs produces a well-structured but ungrounded angle.
- An angle archetype is a starting lens, not evidence the resulting angle will work; every archetype-generated angle still requires the hypothesis statement, success signal, guardrail, and test matrix this skill's method already requires.
- Do not force an archetype that does not genuinely fit the evidence; a cultural-relevance or category-challenge angle built on a manufactured or stale premise typically underperforms a more conventional angle grounded in real audience evidence.
- Multiple archetypes applied to the same underlying insight are not automatically distinct strategic tests; confirm a resulting matrix cell actually varies the strategic reason to care, not just its surface expression, per the skill's existing distinction between angle and hook.
